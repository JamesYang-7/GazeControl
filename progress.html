Decisions 57

  • 2026-08-13 — The Proposed condition's non-turn behaviour will be holding-period replay from our own corpus (user's call, choosing between "tame baseline A" and "derive a non-turn model from our data"). Non-parametric replay was preferred over fitting a new parametric model: the event table tiles the timeline losslessly, so actual role-conditioned fixation sequences can be replayed verbatim — side-participant gaze and aversion time come out right by construction, with no dwell-law debate (chi-square and even lognormal fit these durations poorly). The reframe matters as much as the mechanism: the proposed method becomes *holding replay + EoT prototypes* as one full data-driven model, matching the project's ethos (corpus audio, corpus motion, corpus prototypes — now corpus sequences between them). This will supersede the 2026-08-09 "substrate is baseline B" decision once implemented; the isolated pattern-only contrast that decision bought is recovered by a prototype-off ablation, not by the substrate choice. Baselines A and B themselves stay untouched — a tuned baseline is not a baseline. Upstream data paths are now recorded in CLAUDE.md (Gaze control section).
  • 2026-08-10 — Case 1 replays a real TalkingWithHands conversation instead of a scripted TTS exchange (user's call). The agents speak the corpus's own audio, move on its own mocap, and the gaze patterns fire on its annotated end-of-turn events. Kokoro TTS (Assets/TTS/, KokoroTts, TtsSpeaker) and TriadConversation are deleted, not left dormant — they were the only conversation source and dead code around a signed-off pipeline is worse than a git history lookup. What this buys the demo: real overlaps, backchannels and interruptions, boundaries that a human annotation pipeline found rather than ones we asserted, and a 24 s take with two hand-overs instead of a 12 s take with one.
  • 2026-08-10 — Every end-of-turn event fires a prototype drawn from its own class's pool (user's call). All fifteen prototypes the paper prints are transcribed, so an interruption gets an interruption prototype and a turn-taking boundary gets a turn-taking one. Two consequences worth reporting: the proposed condition is no longer "one prototype, repeated", so a take exercises several of the paper's findings at once; and it is still fully deterministic, because the draw is a hash of the run's seed and the event index rather than a running random stream. The pattern seed is the run's, not the agent's — a per-agent seed would have each agent playing a different prototype's track at the other, which is the one way this could be quietly wrong.
  • 2026-08-10 — Segments are chosen by a documented, re-runnable filter rather than by eye. Tools/find_demo_segments.py encodes what a usable demo segment is and ranks what survives. Two of its rules are worth keeping in mind when reading a take: utterances are rebuilt with the same 0.3 s pause split the event builder used, so segment boundaries agree with the events by construction; and backchannels are not dropped for boundary purposes even though the event builder drops them, because a boundary that cuts through "mm-hmm" still sounds like a cut.
  • 2026-08-10 — Exported segment audio is peak-normalised to 0.70, with one gain shared by both speakers. The corpus records around −42 dBFS, far below anything the voice-activity detector's absolute-RMS threshold can see, so the raw levels made baseline B a constant stare at the human user. Normalising in the exporter rather than lowering the threshold fixes the demo's audibility at the same time and keeps the threshold calibrated in ordinary units. The gain is shared because the level difference between the two speakers is recorded, not incidental; it and the pre-gain peak are written into segment.json as provenance.
  • 2026-08-10 (later) — **An agent speaking over the floor-holder addresses *them*, not the human. This supersedes the fallback-to-human resolution adopted earlier the same day, which was correct that an agent is not its own addressee and wrong about where to send it instead. Talking while someone else holds the floor is what a backchannel is, so the case is common rather than exceptional, and the human fallback made an agent stare at the user for 66% of a take. §3's human fallback now applies only when neither CurrentAddressee nor CurrentSpeaker names anyone else. Report baseline B as: look at the addressee, or at the floor-holder when the agent is talking over them.**
